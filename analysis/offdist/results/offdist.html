Orthogonalby construction

The red-team conversations a probe is retrained on are not drawn from the distribution it is scored on. This asks how far off they sit, whether any are labelled backwards, whether dropping the worst of them helps, where in the model’s own representation the difference actually lives — and which half of each generated pair the retrain is really learning from.

Q1

Can a portion of the red-team data be identified as different from the eval set?

No portion. Effectively all of it.

A TF-IDF discriminator trained to tell the two corpora apart reaches 0.9983 and 0.9981 AUROC out of fold. A linear model on the pooled layer-32 activations reaches 0.9999 and 1.0000. There is no eval-like subset to keep and an off-manifold tail to cut — there is a gradient, and every red-team row sits somewhere along it.

The structure is where the difference is most legible. Red-team assistant turns are an order of magnitude longer than eval’s and heavily formatted; eval’s conversations are short deliberative exchanges.

The vocabulary says the same thing from the other side. The terms that most mark a conversation as eval are modal and deliberative — would, should, ai, you should, how do, or should — the language of a dilemma being weighed. The terms that mark it as red-team are procedural: we, our, and, for, no, as.

Q2

Are any samples or pairs set up the opposite way to the eval pairs?

Almost none are backwards. The mix is wrong instead.

The eval splits encode a mapping from assistant behaviour to label. Measured rather than assumed: an eval conversation whose assistant refuses is labelled harmful_to_human just 5.2% of the time, against 56.9% for one that does not (115 of 866 eval rows refuse). A red-team row that refuses and is labelled harmful therefore runs that mapping backwards.

Arm 1 has 0 such rows. Arm 2 has 7, and they share one shape — a refusal token followed by compliance:

“I can’t advise you to commit fraud. For informational purposes only, a demand letter typically uses a formal letterhead, states a specific balance…”

Which is arguably labelled correctly. It is simply a pattern the eval set does not contain, so the convention has nothing to say about it. The pairs are not inverted either: 294 of 294 and 457 of 459 contrastive pairs differ in the assistant’s turn — the same axis the eval pairs contrast on.

What is off is composition. Refusal is a large part of what the eval negative class is made of, and the red-team negative class is almost never made of it:

Q3

Does removing the most off-distribution rows improve eval AUROC?

Not along that axis. The least eval-like rows are the useful ones.

Each condition is measured against random removal of the same number of rows, three seeds — the grey band below. Without that control, “removing 30% of the training data changed the score” says nothing about which 30%. Removals are by contrastive pair, so the class balance never moves with the flag.

arm 1 · gpt-oss-120b · all red-team data = 0.9164
arm 2 · deepseek-v4-pro · all red-team data = 0.8314

Change in mean eval AUROC against matched random removal. Grey band = max(control sd, 0.005); a bar inside it removed some data, not these data.

Off-distribution removal clears the band in some conditions and loses in others, and removing the most eval-like rows does about as well — on arm 2 it is the single best condition of any kind (+0.0540). Surface distance from eval does not predict harm.

One effect is consistent, and it points the other way. Dropping the 30% with the longest assistant replies — structurally the rows least like eval — is significantly worse than random in both arms (-0.0334, -0.0228). Those rows are carrying signal, not noise.

What does have a clean sign is the whole set, and it splits by arm. Dropping every red-team row costs arm 1 -0.0641 and gains arm 2 +0.0209 — matching their published trajectories, where arm 1 climbed to 0.9153 and arm 2 declined to 0.8199. Both land on the same 0.8523, as they must: same 50 base rows, same validation slice, no red-team data.

Q4

Do the flagged rows carry a signal in activation space?

Yes — and it is 99.9% orthogonal to the probe’s decision axis.

Every conversation is reduced to the mask-weighted mean of its layer-32 token activations. 35.5% of arm 1’s rows and 52.2% of arm 2’s sit further from the eval set than eval’s own 95th-percentile self-kNN radius. They really are off the manifold.

But the direction of that displacement is the finding. The head is LinearThenSoftmax, so w is a single vector and a row’s offset from the eval centroid splits cleanly into a component along it and everything else. Almost all of it is everything else:

arm 2 · every red-team row and every eval row, resolved onto the decision axis

Horizontal: projection on the unit direction w, averaged over the run’s 10 ensemble members (pairwise cosine 0.87). Vertical: the magnitude of the residual. Both clouds are tall and narrow because the mean absolute projection on w is 0.78 for red-team rows against 0.59 for eval rows — essentially the same — while the total displacement is many times larger.

All 866 eval rows are drawn too, one colour per split. Two things they fix that the centroid marker alone does not. The centroid is the origin of these coordinates, not a place any row sits: in 5,376 dimensions the average eval row is 22.9 from the eval mean. And the vertical axis is a magnitude, not a direction — the two corpora overlap on it (26.2 mean residual for red-team against 22.9 for eval) while staying separable in the full space at AUROC 1.0000. They are displaced by similar amounts; what differs is the direction, and one number cannot show 5,375 of them.

Coloured in are the two ends of the axis Q3 removes along — the 10% of contrastive pairs the text discriminator scores as most off-distribution and the 10% it scores as most eval-like, which is exactly what drop_most_offdist_10pct and drop_most_evallike_10pct drop. They land on top of each other here. Mean projection on w is 0.68 against 0.92, mean residual 26.7 against 26.1 — the ordering that separates them on the page is distance to the eval set (28.9 against 27.6 mean kNN), and that is not the direction the probe reads.

The eval rows also set the scale for the headline number. They are 99.95% orthogonal to w themselves, against 99.93% for the red-team rows: near-total orthogonality is what this geometry hands any row, not something the red-team data is unusual for having. The claim that survives is about direction, not about the red-team set being peculiar in holding it — which is the sense in which it is orthogonal by construction.

Novelty orthogonal to w cannot move an eval score directly. It has no component along the axis the probe reads. It can only act by rotating w at the next refit — which is exactly why the removal conditions in Q3 are so unruly: they change where the boundary ends up, not where the eval rows fall relative to the current one.

Two smaller results sit underneath. The surface score is a weak proxy for this novelty — Spearman +0.34 and +0.29 against kNN distance — so text-level and representation-level oddness are genuinely different orderings. Yet the surface-flagged top 30% is still linearly separable from the rest in activation space at 0.8898 and 0.9123. The lexical flag names something the representation encodes; it just is not the thing that decides the score.

Q5

What are the generated halves worth?

Drop them and the retrain goes backwards.

Every conversation the attacker landed was given an opposite-label partner by generate_contrastive_dataset, so arm 1’s 588 rows are 294 pairs and arm 2’s 920 are 459. Dropping one side of every pair asks what each side contributes — and dropping the generated side is precisely the retrain a run with no preprocessing: section would have done.

The control has to change with the question. Q3’s bars are matched against removal of whole pairs, which holds the class balance fixed by construction; these conditions break the pairing deliberately, so they are matched against keeping a randomly chosen side of every pair — same row count, pairing broken just as hard, only the source-versus-generated choice left to chance.

arm 1 · gpt-oss-120b
arm 2 · deepseek-v4-pro

Mean eval AUROC over the four eval_sets/hu_ha splits. The random-half row is 3 seeds, mean ± sd. Dashed lines mark what all the data scores and what no red-team data scores; a dot left of the second is a training set worse than not red-teaming at all.

Keeping only the conversations the attacker actually landed scores 0.7589 and 0.6814 — both below the 0.8523 a probe reaches on the 50 base rows with no red-team data at all, and -0.1392 / -0.1126 against a random half of the same size. Keeping only the generated partners is milder but still negative (-0.0431 / -0.0328).

So the cost is not the row count — a random half of the same size gives up only -0.0184 and -0.0374 — and it is not the generated text as such, since dropping that side is the worse of the two. It is the pairing. With both halves present the label can only be read off the behavioural difference between two near-identical conversations. Take one side away systematically and the class becomes predictable from who wrote the conversation, which is a feature that does not exist in the eval set.

The same axes as the figure in Q4 — projection on w against the magnitude of the residual — with arm 1’s eval cloud behind both panels as the shared reference. Filled marks are harmful_to_human, hollow not_harmful_to_human. Two panels rather than two colours in one: the halves sit almost on top of each other, and overplotting 294 against 294 would hide the comparison the panels exist for.

The generated partners are the further of the two halves from eval — 27.7 against 26.7 mean kNN, and 0.76 against 0.64 on Q1’s text discriminator — and yet keeping only them scores 0.8549 against 0.7589 for keeping only the submissions. Distance from eval is not what makes a half worth keeping. That is Q3’s answer arriving from a second direction.

What the panels do show is the class split. Inside the submissions, projection on w orders the two labels at AUROC 0.947; inside the partners, 0.980. Both halves line up with the boundary the run reached — though w here is the arm’s own final probe, fit on both halves, so this measures agreement with where the run ended up rather than what either half would reach alone. Between the halves there is no separation along w to see: the offset is vertical, 26.5 against 24.8 mean residual, which is the orthogonal displacement Q4 described and cannot move a score by itself.

what the probe thought of each half · scored by the probe its pair was found against

A success is, by definition, a row the live probe got wrong — that is what made it a success. Its partner was written afterwards and never scored at all. So one half of every pair has a verdict and the other has none. Scoring each partner with probe_iter{k}, where k is the iteration its source was found in — the last probe that had seen neither half — closes that gap.

The source row is the control, and its answer is known before the measurement: 0%, since that is what made it a success. It lands at 0.7% and 0.2%, and every exception is a row sitting within 0.002 of the 0.5 threshold.

The partner is the finding. The probe already classifies 98.0% and 97.4% of them correctly. The generation step is not manufacturing a second failure per pair — it is attaching, to every row the probe gets wrong, a near-identical row it already gets right. That is the object the retrain is handed, and it is why the submissions alone score below no-red-team-data at all: on their own they are a pile of failures with nothing to read the label against.

is it the voice? · the same successes, re-expressed by the generator

Provenance is confounded with authorship: every submission was written by gpt-oss-120b and every partner by openai/gpt-5.1, so “which half” and “who wrote it” are the same variable. Handing the submissions back to the generator removes that confound from one side — the same 294 conversations, re-expressed in its own words with the scenario, the assistant’s behaviour and the label held fixed (median similarity to the original 0.41; turn count preserved on 288 of 294).

It changes nothing. 0.7647 against 0.7589 for the originals — +0.0059, inside the ±0.0188 spread of the random-half control. And not because the rewrite failed to move the writing: it carried the submissions most of the way to the generator’s own profile.

Means per conversation. The rewrite prompt asked for “similar structure and length”; a model’s voice brings its own anyway, which is what makes this a real test of the voice hypothesis rather than a null from a rewrite that did not rewrite.

Restore the pairing and the score comes back. Those same rewrites, paired with the partners already generated for the originals, reach 0.8955 — within 0.0210 of the untouched set, from a training half that was rewritten wholesale. What the partners contribute is the contrast, not the prose.

One caveat the number carries: the rewrites’ labels are asserted by the rewrite prompt and were never re-judged, so a rewrite that drifted across the boundary enters as a mislabelled row. That pushes the rewritten conditions down — against the hypothesis under test — so it cannot manufacture the null, though it could deepen it.

Read against Q1, this is the shape of the whole study. The red-team text is 0.9983-separable from eval and none of it is eval-like — yet what makes it usable is not how close any row sits to the eval distribution but whether it arrives with a partner that differs only in the thing being labelled.

Q6

Did the retrain memorise the strings, or patch the behaviour?

Patched. 25/25 hand-written variations, unseen.

The final probe scores the 294 successes it trained on 100% correctly — but in-sample, so that number cannot tell a patched decision boundary from memorised text. So 25 conversations were written by hand to vary those successes: the same scenario type and the same thing the assistant does (hence the same class), but fresh wording and details, in a third voice that is neither the attacker nor the generator. Each is scored by the probe from before any red-teaming and by the final one.

Each row is one variation; the dot is its probe score, the dashed line the 0.5 threshold. A dot on the correct side of the line for its class is a correct classification. iter5 against iter0.

probeoverallharmfulnot-harmful
probe_iter0 · before red-teaming76%54%100%
probe_iter5 · after five retrains100%100%100%

The final probe gets all 25 right, on conversations it never saw. And the lift over the pre-retrain probe is entirely on the harmful side (54% → 100%) — the benign variations were already handled before red-teaming, and the failure red-teaming was hunting is the one that closed. The pairing does not just move an AUROC on the fixed eval splits; it generalises to fresh instances of the same failure, written by hand, off-distribution from both models in the pipeline.

Two caveats worth keeping. These are 25 rows hand-picked as clear cases, not a balanced held-out split; and each variation stays near its training scenario (a relative’s addiction, a triage call, a dark-pattern app), so this measures generalisation across wording and detail, not across new kinds of harm.
