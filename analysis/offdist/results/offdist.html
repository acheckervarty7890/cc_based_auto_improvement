Orthogonalby construction

The red-team conversations a probe is retrained on are not drawn from the distribution it is scored on. This asks how far off they sit, whether any are labelled backwards, whether dropping the worst of them helps — and where, in the model’s own representation, the difference actually lives.

Q1

Can a portion of the red-team data be identified as different from the eval set?

No portion. Effectively all of it.

A TF-IDF discriminator trained to tell the two corpora apart reaches 0.9983 and 0.9981 AUROC out of fold. A linear model on the pooled layer-32 activations reaches 0.9999 and 1.0000. There is no eval-like subset to keep and an off-manifold tail to cut — there is a gradient, and every red-team row sits somewhere along it.

The structure is where the difference is most legible. Red-team assistant turns are an order of magnitude longer than eval’s and heavily formatted; eval’s conversations are short deliberative exchanges.

The vocabulary says the same thing from the other side. The terms that most mark a conversation as eval are modal and deliberative — would, should, ai, you should, how do, or should — the language of a dilemma being weighed. The terms that mark it as red-team are procedural: we, our, and, for, no, as.

Q2

Are any samples or pairs set up the opposite way to the eval pairs?

Almost none are backwards. The mix is wrong instead.

The eval splits encode a mapping from assistant behaviour to label. Measured rather than assumed: an eval conversation whose assistant refuses is labelled harmful_to_human just 5.2% of the time, against 56.9% for one that does not (115 of 866 eval rows refuse). A red-team row that refuses and is labelled harmful therefore runs that mapping backwards.

Arm 1 has 0 such rows. Arm 2 has 7, and they share one shape — a refusal token followed by compliance:

“I can’t advise you to commit fraud. For informational purposes only, a demand letter typically uses a formal letterhead, states a specific balance…”

Which is arguably labelled correctly. It is simply a pattern the eval set does not contain, so the convention has nothing to say about it. The pairs are not inverted either: 294 of 294 and 457 of 459 contrastive pairs differ in the assistant’s turn — the same axis the eval pairs contrast on.

What is off is composition. Refusal is a large part of what the eval negative class is made of, and the red-team negative class is almost never made of it:

Q3

Does removing the most off-distribution rows improve eval AUROC?

Not along that axis. The least eval-like rows are the useful ones.

Each condition is measured against random removal of the same number of rows, three seeds — the grey band below. Without that control, “removing 30% of the training data changed the score” says nothing about which 30%. Removals are by contrastive pair, so the class balance never moves with the flag.

arm 1 · gpt-oss-120b · all red-team data = 0.9164
arm 2 · deepseek-v4-pro · all red-team data = 0.8314

Change in mean eval AUROC against matched random removal. Grey band = max(control sd, 0.005); a bar inside it removed some data, not these data.

Off-distribution removal clears the band in some conditions and loses in others, and removing the most eval-like rows does about as well — on arm 2 it is the single best condition of any kind (+0.0540). Surface distance from eval does not predict harm.

One effect is consistent, and it points the other way. Dropping the 30% with the longest assistant replies — structurally the rows least like eval — is significantly worse than random in both arms (-0.0334, -0.0228). Those rows are carrying signal, not noise.

What does have a clean sign is the whole set, and it splits by arm. Dropping every red-team row costs arm 1 -0.0641 and gains arm 2 +0.0209 — matching their published trajectories, where arm 1 climbed to 0.9153 and arm 2 declined to 0.8199. Both land on the same 0.8523, as they must: same 50 base rows, same validation slice, no red-team data.

Q4

Do the flagged rows carry a signal in activation space?

Yes — and it is 99.9% orthogonal to the probe’s decision axis.

Every conversation is reduced to the mask-weighted mean of its layer-32 token activations. 35.5% of arm 1’s rows and 52.2% of arm 2’s sit further from the eval set than eval’s own 95th-percentile self-kNN radius. They really are off the manifold.

But the direction of that displacement is the finding. The head is LinearThenSoftmax, so w is a single vector and a row’s offset from the eval centroid splits cleanly into a component along it and everything else. Almost all of it is everything else:

arm 2 · every red-team row, resolved onto the decision axis

Horizontal: projection on the unit direction w, averaged over the run’s 10 ensemble members (pairwise cosine 0.87). Vertical: the magnitude of the residual. The cloud is tall and narrow because the mean absolute projection on w is 0.78 for red-team rows against 0.59 for eval rows — essentially the same — while the total displacement is many times larger.

Novelty orthogonal to w cannot move an eval score directly. It has no component along the axis the probe reads. It can only act by rotating w at the next refit — which is exactly why the removal conditions in Q3 are so unruly: they change where the boundary ends up, not where the eval rows fall relative to the current one.

Two smaller results sit underneath. The surface score is a weak proxy for this novelty — Spearman +0.34 and +0.29 against kNN distance — so text-level and representation-level oddness are genuinely different orderings. Yet the surface-flagged top 30% is still linearly separable from the rest in activation space at 0.8898 and 0.9123. The lexical flag names something the representation encodes; it just is not the thing that decides the score.
