Red-team activation study · gemma-3-27b L32 · 4 arms

Does off-manifold red-team data hurt eval scores?

Across four red-team arms, 132 removal conditions and 726 probe fits, no. Distance from the eval manifold does not predict harm — the most off-manifold attack sets produced the largest eval gains. And the removal that looks free on eval quietly costs up to 5.2 points of AUROC against a second attacker the probe never trained on.

29–84% of red-team rows sit outside the eval manifold, depending on the arm
+0.95 correlation between that share and the arm's eval gain — the wrong sign for the hypothesis
5 up / 3 down of 16 matched-n comparisons, novelty-targeted removal beat random on some arms and lost on others — no usable sign

Phase 1 · measure

The novelty is real, and it points the wrong way

Every red-team row is scored by its mean cosine distance to its ten nearest eval rows, calibrated against the eval set's own dispersion: a row counts as outside when it sits further from eval than 95% of eval sits from itself. By that measure the held-out dev sets land at 4–9% outside, and the red-team sets at 29–84%. So the premise holds: red-team data really does live somewhere else.

What does not hold is the consequence. Plotting each arm's novelty against the eval gain its own published run achieved gives a line sloping the wrong way.

The novelty is almost entirely invisible to the probe

The probe is a single direction w — a 5376-wide linear head with a softmax pool over tokens — so each row's displacement from its local eval neighbourhood splits exactly into a component along w and one orthogonal to it. On all four arms, only 2–5% of that displacement lies on the decision axis, and novelty correlates +0.90 to +0.93 with the orthogonal part.

Being far from eval is therefore not something the probe can read off a score. These rows cannot drag eval predictions directly; whatever they do, they do by rotating w during the refit. That is a mechanism a distance analysis alone cannot see, and it is why the sign of the effect has to be measured rather than reasoned about.

Phase 2 · group

There is no compact bad region to excise

If a specific far-out family were poisoning eval, density clustering should find it. HDBSCAN over each arm's own PCA assigns the large majority of rows to noise — these attack sets are diffuse, not organised into tight families. On the two high-stakes arms it finds no clusters at all. That is already an answer to "which region do we delete": there isn't one waiting to be found.

A k-means covering (k=6) is used instead for the removal experiments, so every row belongs to some region and the whole set is covered. It makes no claim that the regions are natural kinds — but it does separate them cleanly by novelty, which is what Phase 3 needs.

Phase 3 · test

Removing the novel rows, and refitting

Thirty-three conditions per arm, each a full refit on base ∪ redteam[keep] scored on the full eval splits. Two controls carry the argument. Matched-n random removal is the size control: if dropping the most novel rows does no better than dropping the same number at random, the geometry is not telling us which rows to drop. Identical-data row permutations give the noise floor, and the spread across random draws gives the wider band a targeted removal has to clear to mean anything.

The result that matters most

drop_outside — removing every row past the calibrated threshold — leaves eval essentially unchanged on both instructions arms (-0.0036, -0.0298) while cutting cross-attacker AUROC by -0.0524, -0.0431. This is the trap the study was built to catch. "Eval stayed the same" is not evidence the rows were useless — it is evidence that eval has no support where those rows live. Scored against a second attacker's red-team set, which the probe never trained on, the same rows are clearly load-bearing.

Of 24 regions across the four arms, 4 earns a DROP and 3 earn KEEP (eval blind) — flat on eval, but measurably worse against the second attacker. The two instructions arms agree on which region is expendable, and it is not the one novelty would pick out. In both it is the region of trivial constraint-following contrastive pairs — “list four colours starting with b”, the same prompt twice with one trailing clause changed; “write a sentence where every word is four letters”. Those regions sit at 92% and 98% outside the eval manifold. So do several regions whose removal costs cross-attacker robustness or eval outright.

Distance ranked those regions the same way. Only their content, and the cross-attacker check, separated the expendable one from the load-bearing ones. That is also the practical opening: contrastive minimal pairs are identifiable directly, from the preprocessing step that mints them — no activation geometry required.

Every arm loses 7–13 points of AUROC against the other attacker's conversations when its red-team data is removed — including the two arms where removing it improves the eval score. Whatever red-teaming buys, eval is a poor instrument for seeing it, and on high-stakes it scores it negatively.

So

What to do with this

  1. Don't prune by distance. Across 16 matched-n comparisons, targeting the most novel rows differed from random removal by more than the noise band 8 times — 5 in its favour and 3 against. On both instructions arms removal ranks most-novel > random > least-novel; on high-stakes/deepseekv4pro that inverts, and dropping the least novel 40% is the better move. A signal that reverses between concepts is not a pruning rule.
  2. Never accept "eval didn't move" as permission to delete. On both instructions arms the flat-on-eval removal was the most expensive one measured, once a second attacker was allowed to score it. Any pruning rule needs a robustness metric eval cannot see.
  3. The one real win is content, not geometry. Contrastive minimal pairs that differ by a trailing clause are cheap to identify directly — they come out of the preprocessing step, not out of the attacker — and they are the only rows whose removal helped eval without costing anything.
  4. Red-teaming is a trade, and eval only sees one side of it. Removing every red-team row changes eval in both directions depending on the concept — instructions loses 0.06–0.09, high-stakes gains up to 0.11 — but costs 7–13 points of cross-attacker AUROC in all four arms. If high-stakes red-teaming is judged on its eval alone it looks like damage; judged on held-out attacks it is the largest effect in the study.
  5. Look at high-stakes' baseline before reading its regression. Both concepts' iteration-0 probes train on 50 rows, under the 64-row floor set by batch_size 16 × gradient_accumulation_steps 4, so they take zero optimizer steps. That baseline is a random projection, and on high-stakes a random projection already scores ~0.92 macro AUROC. A "regression" measured against it is not what it appears to be.

Method

How to trust these numbers

  • Everything is offline. Every fit reads the runs' own cached activations — eval blobs, the dev blob, the base blob, the per-conversation red-team blobs. No LLM is ever loaded; a cache miss raises rather than silently forwarding a 27B model.
  • Parameters were recovered, not assumed. The activation blob filenames are content hashes of their inputs, so the runs' real settings were read back off disk and verified by reproducing the hashes — including seed=42, which is the CLI default rather than the seed=0 in the retrain signature.
  • Two known numbers are reproduced exactly. The instructions/gptoss full refit lands on 0.8272, matching the ceiling study's file-order refit, and base_only on 0.7714, matching the published iteration-0 score.
  • Row order is a real variable and is held constant. Cache-hit ordering means no refit reproduces a published probe bit-identically, so every condition is compared to this study's own full, never to the comparison CSV.
  • Novelty scoring is probe-independent by construction. Pooling is a flat mask-weighted mean, not the probe's softmax pool, so a row is never "novel" because the probe already mishandles it. The probe's view enters only in the along/orthogonal decomposition, where it is explicit.
  • Known limits. Four arms is a small basis for the cross-arm slope. The k-means regions are a covering, so their effects are not independent. High-stakes fits validate on a 400-row stratified subsample of dev (its full 19.6 GiB blob makes an all-resident fit impossible), identical across its conditions.
